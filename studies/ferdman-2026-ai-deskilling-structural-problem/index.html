    AI deskilling is a structural problem¶

    Author: Avigail Ferdman Journal: ai-society (2026) 41:3001–3013 DOI: 10.1007/s00146-025-02686-z

    Abstract¶

    Many artificial intelligence tools replace or stand to replace human activity, via automated decision-making, recommender systems and content generation. The more artificial intelligence (AI) replaces valuable human activity, the more it risks deskilling humans of their human capacities. This paper argues for applying a structural perspective to this phenomenon. It introduces the concept of ‘capacity-hostile environments’ to identify instances where AI mediation impedes human capacity cultivation. The analysis moves beyond individual responsibility that agents have to cultivate their human capacities, demonstrating how AI’s influence creates systemic conditions that could inhibit the development and exercise of human capacities by undermining the process of capacity cultivation. Drawing on the philosophy of skill as well as social epistemology, this paper argues that capacity cultivation (skilling) includes acquiring agential control over the capacities, inculcated through a long, gradual process of habituation. Habituation, in turn, depends on learning from others: the ‘know how’ of the skill, as well as a shared understanding of the value of the skill. AI mediation risks undermining the quality of the conditions for capacity habituation, leading to capacity impoverishment. By exploring the role of AI in mediating human activity, the paper highlights the need to evaluate AI applications based on their conduciveness or hostility to capacity cultivation. Ultimately, it calls for a critical reflection on the values inherent in AI socio-technical systems and emphasizes the societal obligation to foster capacity-conducive environments in the age of AI.

    Key Arguments & Framework¶

    1. Capacity-Deskilling vs. Skill Obsolescence¶

    The paper distinguishes between the obsolescence of specific skills (e.g., coding, basket-weaving), which is a normal part of technological progress, and the deskilling of core human capacities. Capacity-deskilling is harmful because it diminishes us as humans, undermining our abilities in the ‘arts of personhood’ (Vallor 2015): the epistemic, social, moral, and creative capacities required to achieve competency in our exercise of practical reason, self-worth, and mutual respect.

    2. Core Human Capacities & Developmental Perfectionism¶

    Drawing on developmental-perfectionism, the paper outlines four core human capacities essential for flourishing: - Epistemic Capacities: Theoretical rationality (thinking, forming beliefs) and practical rationality (phronesis/wisdom). - Social Capacities: Moral capacity, and the capacity for friendship and love. - Creative Capacities: Making unfamiliar combinations of familiar ideas to transform conceptual spaces. - Volitional Capacity: The ability to exert effort, overcome difficulty, and persevere to achieve intrinsically valuable goals. Treated as a meta-capacity necessary for developing the others.

    3. The Philosophy of Skill: Agential Control & Habituation¶

    Becoming skilled requires two key properties: - Agential Control: The ability to adjust the execution of an action as it unfolds. Skills require control over initiating the task, distinguishing them from habits which are triggered by environmental cues. - Habituation: A gradual process of becoming skilled through practice, not just testimony. It involves two critical components: - Intersubjectivity: Learning from mentors and social practices that instill both the 'know-how' and the shared valuing of the skill. - Embodiment: Physical presence and sensory experience are critical for recognizing others as moral agents and forming meaningful relationships. Disembodied interaction requires more mental effort to attribute moral agency.

    4. Structural Dimension: Affordances & Environments¶

    The paper argues that deskilling is a structural problem, not merely an individual ethical failing. The environment one inhabits is a 'world of affordances' (action possibilities). - Capacity-Conducive Environments: Afford opportunities for embodied, intersubjective habituation. They encourage the capacity to will and provide public goods (libraries, community centers) that foster shared experiences. - Capacity-Hostile Environments: Restrict, limit, or create a narrow field of affordances. They afford opportunities for shallow skilling while restricting fuller development. Automation and AI tools can create these environments by offloading tasks, leading to automation bias, atrophied judgment, and a narrow field of affordances that discourages users from seeking alternative avenues to exercise their capacities.

    5. Case Study: Artificial Personal Assistants (APAs)¶

    APAs (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) are analyzed as potential capacity-hostile environments, particularly regarding life-planning. - Life-Planning as Self-Constituting: Planning is not just instrumental; it constitutes personal identity and character. Outsourcing it to an APA undermines the development of practical wisdom and agential control. - Plan-Forming vs. Plan-Following: Forming a plan requires self-deliberation, settling, and coordinating (exercising epistemic, moral, social, and creative capacities). Following an APA's plan is more habitual, triggered by the tool's output. - Erosion of Intersubjectivity & Embodiment: Heavy reliance on APAs decreases embodied, intersubjective interaction with humans, replacing shared experiences with algorithmic recommendations. This undermines the shared valuing of capacities and limits the development of social and moral capacities. - The "Always-On" Trap: APAs are designed to be constantly available and helpful, inviting users to become plan-followers. This constant availability limits the user's ability to develop independence and practical wisdom gained through real-world experience and failure.

    Implications for Market Research & Insights¶

    The structural perspective on AI deskilling provides a critical lens for evaluating AI tools in the market research industry. Rather than focusing solely on efficiency gains, organizations must assess whether AI implementations create capacity-hostile environments that erode the epistemic, social, and creative capacities of researchers. Preserving agential-control and fostering habituation-process through capacity-conducive-environments are essential for maintaining the professional expertise and human flourishing of insights practitioners.
