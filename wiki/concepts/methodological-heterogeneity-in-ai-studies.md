# Methodological Heterogeneity in AI Studies

## Definition

The challenge that **different AI adoption measurement approaches yield dramatically different findings** about AI's economic impact. As demonstrated by the contrast between exposure-based studies and spending-based research like the [[ramp-revelio-2026-ai-jobs-impact-study]], methodological choices fundamentally shape conclusions about AI's labor market effects.

## Evidence of Heterogeneity

| Study Type | Primary Method | Key Employment Finding | Example |
|------------|----------------|------------------------|---------|
| **Exposure-based** | Occupational AI exposure scores | Negative impact on entry-level jobs | Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) |
| **Spending-based** | Observed AI vendor payments | Positive impact at high intensity | Ramp-Revelio study |
| **Survey-based** | Executive adoption surveys | Mixed or positive impacts | Yotzov et al. (2026) |

The Ramp-Revelio study documents this heterogeneity through direct comparison:
- Exposure-based studies predict job losses in high-exposure occupations
- Spending-based research shows job growth among high-intensity adopters
- The discrepancy stems from methodological differences, not contradictory realities

## Root Causes

Three fundamental methodological differences drive these divergent findings:

### 1. Adoption Measurement

- **Exposure scores**: Measure theoretical task feasibility ("could AI do this?")
- **Spending data**: Measure actual adoption behavior ("is the firm paying for AI?")
- **Surveys**: Capture self-reported usage ("do executives say they use AI?")

As the Ramp-Revelio study notes: "These estimates are closer to the Ramp–Revelio paid-adoption series than the firm-weighted BTOS benchmark, but they still measure a different object."

### 2. Intensity Dimension

- **Exposure scores**: Typically binary or quintile-based
- **Spending data**: Reveals continuous intensity effects (PEPM metric)
- **Surveys**: Often lack precise intensity measures

The Ramp-Revelio study found only high-intensity adopters showed significant employment growth (+10.2%), while low-intensity adopters showed no change.

### 3. Selection Bias Handling

- **Exposure scores**: Cannot distinguish adopters from non-adopters with identical workforce
- **Spending data**: Enables direct comparison of adopters vs. later adopters
- **Surveys**: Often lack pre-adoption data for comparison

The Ramp-Revelio study documents substantial pre-adoption differences that confound simple adopter vs. non-adopter comparisons.

## Implications for Market Research

This methodological heterogeneity creates critical challenges:

- **Conflicting guidance**: Different studies recommend opposite actions based on methodology
- **Implementation uncertainty**: Hard to predict AI impact without knowing intensity threshold
- **Evaluation complexity**: Must select appropriate methodology for specific context
- **Vendor claims**: AI providers may cite favorable methodology while ignoring others

## Strategic Response Framework

Market research organizations should adopt a methodologically sophisticated approach:

1. **Triangulate findings**: Consider evidence from multiple methodological approaches
2. **Assess intensity**: Focus on high-intensity implementation for meaningful impact
3. **Control for selection**: Account for pre-existing differences when evaluating AI
4. **Contextualize results**: Recognize sector-specific effects (e.g., concentrated in Information)

## Practical Guidance

For market research professionals, navigating methodological heterogeneity requires:
- Never relying on a single study's methodology for decision-making
- Understanding the measurement approach behind any AI impact claim
- Recognizing that "AI adoption" has no single empirical rate (as shown in Ramp-Revelio Figure 1)
- Focusing on high-intensity implementation thresholds rather than binary adoption

Without this methodological sophistication, organizations risk making decisions based on incomplete or misleading evidence about AI's true impact.
