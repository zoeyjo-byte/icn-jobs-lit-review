# [[intersubjectivity-in-skilling]]

## Definition
The shared understanding dimension of skill development, where learning occurs through relationships with others who guide and share the value of the skill. Becoming skilled typically requires a mentor to instill not only mechanical aspects but also the value of the skill, as detailed in [[ferdman-2026-ai-deskilling-structural-problem]].

## Key Characteristics
- Involves mentor-mentee relationships with shared intentions
- Requires 'shared valuing' of both the skill and its outputs
- Learning occurs through social practices at societal scale
- Enables understanding of a skill's constitutive aim or telos
- Critical for developing motivation to pursue mastery levels

## Mentorship Dynamics
Effective intersubjective skill development involves:
- Mentors guiding novices into proper practice of the right kind
- Instilling not just mechanical aspects but the value of the skill
- Creating shared history and participatory sense-making
- Developing shared understanding of the skill's purpose
- Gradually transferring responsibility as competence grows

## Societal Dimension
Social practices performed by groups or societies provide mentoring environments:
- Family, colleagues, and citizens create shared valuing contexts
- Social practices generate moral reasons for action through constitutive aims (Manne 2013)
- Learning occurs 'through the practices of the common life' (MacIntyre 2017)
- Shared understanding develops through embodied interaction

## AI Impact Analysis
AI systems can undermine intersubjective habituation by:
- Replacing human mentors with algorithmic guidance
- Creating environments where users interact primarily with non-human agents
- Reducing opportunities for shared valuing of capacities
- Preventing development of shared understanding about skill value
- Diminishing opportunities for embodied mentorship

## Market Research Implications
- Junior researchers need human mentorship for capacity development
- Organizations should avoid fully automated training systems
- Research teams should maintain opportunities for embodied collaboration
- AI tools should complement rather than replace human mentorship
- Shared valuing of research capacities must be cultivated through human interaction

## Critical Failure Point
When enough persons do not value excellence of a human capacity (e.g., critical thinking), the capacity is in danger of atrophying. Without intersubjective habituation:
- Individuals cannot learn to value masterful levels of capacities
- Shallow skill development becomes entrenched
- Societal devaluation of capacities occurs
- Structural conditions for deskilling are created
