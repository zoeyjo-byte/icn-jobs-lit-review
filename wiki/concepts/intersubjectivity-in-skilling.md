# Intersubjectivity in Skilling

Intersubjectivity in skilling refers to the shared understanding dimension of skill development, where learning occurs through relationships with others who guide, model, and share the value of the skill. This concept is essential for understanding why AI systems that replace human interaction may undermine capacity development.

## Definition and Characteristics

Intersubjectivity in the context of skill development involves:

1. **Shared valuing**: Both mentor and mentee come to view the value of the skill as their own
2. **Guided practice**: A mentor provides appropriate challenges and feedback at each stage of development
3. **Social practices**: Groups or societies create environments that generate reasons for action
4. **Constitutive aims**: Activities have telos that ultimately help people fare well rather than badly

As Kate Manne argues, "social practices are often what generates moral reasons for action, as they have a constitutive aim or telos, which is ultimately the aim of helping people fare well rather than badly."

## Two Levels of Intersubjectivity

### 1. Mentor-Mentee Relationship

- **Guidance**: A good mentor is typically necessary to cultivate the proper motivational structure
- **Value transmission**: Mentors instill not only mechanical aspects of the skill but also its value
- **Shared intentions**: Both mentor and mentee develop aligned goals for skill development

This relationship is crucial because "learning to value a capacity is part of the process of its habituation." Without proper mentoring, learners cannot develop deep appreciation for the skill.

### 2. Societal Scale

- **Social practices**: The family, colleagues, citizens, and other social groups provide mentoring environments
- **Shared norms**: Societies establish expectations about what constitutes skilled performance
- **Collective understanding**: Communities develop shared interpretations of what the skill means

Alasdair MacIntyre noted that "learning is carried out through the practices of the common life," emphasizing how social context shapes skill development.

## AI's Impact on Intersubjectivity

AI systems can undermine intersubjectivity in several ways:

- **Replacing human mentors**: When AI provides guidance without human connection
- **Lacking shared valuing**: AI cannot genuinely share the value of a skill with users
- **Reducing embodied interaction**: Digital interfaces limit the richness of mentor-mentee relationships
- **Creating isolated learning**: Users develop skills without connection to social practices

For instance, when users rely on [[artificial-personal-assistants]] for life planning advice, they miss opportunities to develop shared understanding about values and goals through human relationships.

## Implications for Market Research

For market research professionals, understanding intersubjectivity is crucial for:

- Designing research tools that preserve opportunities for collaborative learning
- Recognizing how AI-augmented tools might affect team dynamics and knowledge sharing
- Understanding how consumer decision-making is shaped by social contexts
- Developing training programs that maintain human connection in skill development
