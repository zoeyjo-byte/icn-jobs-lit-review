# Intersubjectivity in Skilling

**First Observed:** 2026-07-03
**Last Updated:** 2026-07-18

## Definition

Intersubjectivity in skilling refers to the shared understanding dimension of skill development, where learning occurs through relationships with others who guide and share the value of the skill. As established in [[ferdman-2026-ai-deskilling-structural-problem]], this dimension is essential for cultivating human capacities, as it enables the transmission of both technical knowledge and the intrinsic value of the skill.

## Core Components

Intersubjectivity in skilling comprises these critical elements:

### Mentor-Mentee Relationship

- A good mentor is typically necessary to cultivate the proper motivational structure for becoming skilled
- Mentors instill not only mechanical aspects of the skill but, as importantly, the value of the skill
- Through this relationship, the novice eventually comes to view the value of the skill as their own
- Both mentor and mentee develop shared intentions that evolve into "shared valuing" of the skill

### Societal Dimension

- Social practices performed by groups or whole societies provide mentoring environments
- Social practices generate moral reasons for action through constitutive aims or telos
- Learning occurs through "the practices of the common life" as agents become responsive to what skillful action requires
- Communities of practice create shared understanding regarding both the outputs of skilled activity and the skill itself

As Ferdman explains: "The process of habituation is intersubjective also on a societal scale. Social practices performed by groups or whole societies can provide a mentoring environment: the family, colleagues, citizens and so on."

## Examples in Market Research

In market research contexts, intersubjectivity in skilling manifests through:

- Senior researchers guiding junior colleagues through complex analysis techniques while conveying why these approaches matter
- Research teams developing shared understanding of ethical research practices through discussion and reflection
- Professional associations creating communities of practice that transmit tacit knowledge and shared values
- Client-researcher relationships where mutual understanding of research value develops over time
- Peer review processes that provide feedback while reinforcing standards of research excellence

## Threats from AI Systems

AI systems can undermine intersubjectivity in skilling through:

- Replacing human mentors with automated guidance systems that cannot convey the intrinsic value of research skills
- Creating disembodied research environments that limit opportunities for shared valuing of research capacities
- Reducing opportunities for developing shared intentions between researchers through automated collaboration tools
- Diminishing the role of social practices in skill development by providing instant answers rather than encouraging communal learning

## Implications for Market Research

The erosion of intersubjectivity in skilling has significant consequences for the market research industry:

- Reduced transmission of tacit knowledge essential for research excellence
- Weakening of shared understanding regarding research ethics and quality standards
- Diminished development of social capacities necessary for collaborative research
- Erosion of the research community's ability to collectively define and uphold professional values
- Increased difficulty in onboarding new researchers as the [[entry-level-squeeze]] intensifies
