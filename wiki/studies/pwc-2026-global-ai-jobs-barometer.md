# PwC 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer

## Overview

PwC's 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer analyzes more than one billion online job advertisements across six continents using Lightcast data. It presents a two-track model of AI's labor-market effects: AI can professionalise work by automating routine tasks and increasing the need for human expertise, or democratise work by automating more expert tasks and reducing skill barriers.

## Main findings

PwC classifies 22% of advertised jobs as professionalised, 52% as democratised, and 26% as having low AI exposure. Professionalised jobs demand additional skills at twice the rate of democratised jobs: skill demand grew 68% relative to 2018 for professionalised roles and 33% for democratised roles. Professionalised job postings grew 39% from 2018 to 2025, compared with 17% for democratised jobs. Average advertised salaries for professionalised roles grew 37% since 2021, compared with 26% for democratised roles, a 42% relative gap.

The barometer also reports that skills in the most AI-exposed jobs are changing 2.2 times faster than in the least exposed jobs, with the gap 75% larger than the previous year. New tasks added to highly exposed occupations are 2.5 times more likely to rely on human-intensive capabilities. These include empathy and emotional intelligence, presence and connectedness, opinion and judgment, creativity, and hope, vision, and leadership. See [[human-intensive-skills]].

## Entry-level implications

The most AI-exposed entry-level roles are seven times more likely than the least exposed to require traditionally senior skills. In highly exposed entry-level jobs, these skills account for 52% of new skill requirements, compared with 7% in the least exposed jobs. Entry-level roles requiring more than ten new traditionally senior skills grew 35% between 2019 and 2025, while non-seniorised roles declined 10%. See [[entry-level-squeeze]] and [[seniorised-roles]].

## Firms, wages, and AI specialists

The most AI-exposed companies showed 33.5% productivity growth from a 2018 baseline, while the top 20% of those companies—the “superstars”—showed 163% growth. Headcount and wages also grew faster at the most exposed companies, although PwC cautions that ORBIS coverage is concentrated among larger formal firms and includes survivorship bias. Workers with AI skills command an average 62% wage premium, and AI-specialist hiring grew eight times faster than total job postings in 2025.

## Analytical framework

PwC proposes four questions for assessing a job's future: how AI changes required expertise; how supply and demand may change; where human oversight or exception handling remains necessary; and how regulation, workflow bottlenecks, and other environmental forces constrain AI use. These questions complement [[ai-professionalization]], [[ai-democratization]], and the [[ai-job-future-framework]].

## Source and methods

The report combines Lightcast job-posting data, ORBIS company data, PwC's AI Occupational Exposure Index, and the EPOCH framework developed by Loaiza and Rigobon. The results should be interpreted as labor-market associations and patterns rather than proof that AI caused every observed change. See [[lightcast]], [[orbis]], [[pwc-ai-occupational-exposure-index]], and [[epoch-score]].
