# AI deskilling is a structural problem

**Author:** Avigail Ferdman  
**Journal:** AI & SOCIETY (2026) 41:3001–3013  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-025-02686-z  
**Affiliation:** Technion – Israel Institute of Technology, Haifa, Israel

## Overview
This paper introduces a structural perspective on technology-induced deskilling, arguing that the erosion of human capacities through AI mediation is not merely an individual ethical failure but a systemic issue rooted in socio-technical design. Drawing on the philosophy of skill, social epistemology, and neo-Aristotelian ethics, the author proposes the concept of **capacity-hostile environments** to describe contexts where AI impedes the cultivation of core human abilities.

## Key Arguments
The study challenges the prevailing narrative that frames deskilling as a matter of individual responsibility. Instead, it posits that humans develop and exercise their epistemic, moral, social, and creative capacities in response to their environment. When AI tools replace valuable human activities—such as decision-making, memory, or moral reasoning—they risk creating environments that are hostile to **capacity cultivation** (skilling). This leads to **capacity impoverishment**, where individuals lose **[[agential-control]]** over their abilities and fail to develop a deep, shared understanding of their value.

The paper analyzes **[[artificial-personal-assistants|APAs]]** as a primary case study. While APAs offer convenience, their constant availability and tendency to offload routine tasks (like **[[life-planning]]** and scheduling) can narrow the user's **field of affordances**. This phenomenon, termed **[[affordance-shrinkage]]**, discourages users from seeking alternative avenues for growth and traps them in a cycle of shallow skilling. The author contrasts this with **capacity-conducive environments** that encourage embodied, intersubjective **[[embodied-habituation]]** and the exercise of the **[[volitional-capacity]]**.
