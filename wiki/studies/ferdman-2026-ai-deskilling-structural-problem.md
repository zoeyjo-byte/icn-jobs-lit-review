# [[ferdman-2026-ai-deskilling-structural-problem]]

## Study Overview

Published in *AI & Society* (2026), Avigail Ferdman's groundbreaking paper establishes that AI-induced deskilling is fundamentally a **structural problem** rather than merely an individual responsibility issue. The study analyzes how AI systems create environments that systematically undermine human capacity development through environmental conditions, not just through individual choices.

## Key Findings

- Introduces the critical distinction between **[[capacity-hostile-environments]]** and **[[capacity-conducive-environments]]**, demonstrating how AI mediation can create conditions that impede human capacity cultivation
- Demonstrates that capacity cultivation (skilling) requires **[[agential-control]]** over activities, developed through a gradual **[[habituation-process]]** that depends on **[[intersubjectivity-in-skilling]]** and **[[embodiment-in-skilling]]**
- Reveals how **[[artificial-personal-assistants]]** (APAs) risk creating capacity-hostile environments by encouraging users to become **[[plan-following]]** rather than **[[plan-forming]]** agents
- Documents how AI automation narrows the **[[narrow-field-of-affords]]** for human capacity development, particularly through offloading routine planning activities
- Establishes that deskilling should be understood as a societal problem requiring structural solutions, not merely an individual failing

## Methodology

The study employs philosophical analysis grounded in neo-Aristotelian **[[developmental-perfectionism]]**, examining the relationship between human capacities and flourishing. It draws on skill philosophy, social epistemology, and affordance theory to develop a framework for evaluating AI systems based on their impact on human capacity development.

## Implications

Ferdman's work fundamentally shifts the discourse on AI and human skills from individual responsibility to structural analysis. It demonstrates that expecting individuals to 'resist deskilling' is unrealistic when AI systems create environments that systematically undermine the conditions necessary for capacity cultivation. The paper calls for societal-level interventions to ensure AI systems are designed to be **[[capacity-conducive-environments]]** rather than **[[capacity-hostile-environments]]**.
