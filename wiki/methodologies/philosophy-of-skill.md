# Philosophy of Skill

The **philosophy of skill** provides a normative and descriptive framework for understanding how humans acquire mastery, develop expertise, and cultivate human capacities. In recent scholarship, it has been applied to analyze the structural impacts of AI on human development, particularly regarding deskilling and capacity impoverishment.

## Core Properties
Two properties of skill are central to this framework:
1. **Agential Control**: The ability to initiate and adjust actions purposefully, distinguishing skilled performance from habitual or automated responses.
2. **Habituation**: The gradual, experiential process through which skills are acquired. Skill cannot be learned solely through testimony or abstract principles; it requires practice, feedback, and engagement with concrete cases.

## Habituation Components
Habituation is further divided into two critical dimensions:
- **Intersubjectivity**: Skill development typically requires mentorship, shared intentions, and a communal understanding of the skill's value. Social practices and communities provide the mentoring environment necessary for cultivating proper motivational structures.
- **Embodiment**: Successful habituation involves participatory sense-making and embodied interaction. Perceiving others as embodied moral agents and engaging in shared physical or social practices is constitutive of learning and valuing skills deeply.

## Application to AI Deskilling
By applying the philosophy of skill to AI systems, researchers can evaluate whether socio-technical environments are conducive or hostile to capacity cultivation. If AI mediation disrupts the habituation process by replacing embodied, intersubjective practice with disembodied, automated responses, it risks causing **[[capacity-hostile-environments]]**. This methodological lens shifts the focus from individual responsibility to structural design, emphasizing the need for AI systems that support rather than replace the gradual, effortful process of becoming skilled.
